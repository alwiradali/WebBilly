Physics

The GCSE physics equations you must know by heart

By Rod Martin8 min readGCSE Combined Science · Y10 & Y11

Physics is the paper where students most often know the science and still lose the marks. Nearly always for the same three reasons: they couldn't recall the equation, they couldn't rearrange it, or they dropped the unit.

All three are fixable in an afternoon.

Recall vs given

Some equations appear on the physics equations sheet in the exam. Others you are expected to recall from memory, and those are where the marks quietly disappear. The recall list is short. Learn it properly and you have removed an entire category of lost marks.

Rearranging: the triangle, and what to do when it doesn't work

For any equation with three quantities, the triangle works: cover the one you want, and what's left is the calculation. For V = I R, cover V and you have I × R; cover I and you have V ÷ R.

The trouble is that plenty of GCSE equations have four terms or a square in them, and triangles collapse. So learn the general method too — it works on everything.

Rearranging, the reliable way

  • Whatever you do to one side, do to the other.
  • To move something that is multiplying, divide both sides by it.
  • To move something that is adding, subtract it from both sides.
  • To undo a square, square-root both sides — and do it last.

So for E = ½mv², to find v: multiply both sides by 2 (2E = mv²), divide by m (2E/m = v²), then square-root (v = √(2E/m)). Three steps, no guessing.

Units are marks

A correct number with no unit does not get the full mark. Neither does a correct number in the wrong unit. Before substituting anything, convert: kilometres to metres, grams to kilograms, minutes to seconds, kilowatts to watts.

Convert first, calculate second. Half the arithmetic errors in GCSE physics are actually conversion errors that happened three lines earlier.

Show your working — properly

Calculation questions carry method marks. That means you can get most of the marks with the wrong final answer, provided the examiner can see what you did. Four lines, every time:

  1. Write the equation in symbols.
  2. Rearrange it if you need to — before you put any numbers in.
  3. Substitute the values, with units.
  4. Give the answer with its unit, and to a sensible number of significant figures.

Graph questions

Two graphs come up relentlessly. On a distance–time graph, the gradient is speed and a flat line means stationary. On a velocity–time graph, the gradient is acceleration and the area under the line is the distance travelled. Mixing those two up costs more marks than almost anything else on the paper.

A ten-minute daily drill

Write the twelve recall equations from memory. Time yourself. Do it every day for a week and it stops being something you revise and becomes something you know — which is the entire objective, because in the exam there is no time to work out what you half-remember.
