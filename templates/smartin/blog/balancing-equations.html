Chemistry

Balancing chemical equations: a method that always works

By Rod Martin7 min readGCSE Combined Science · Y10 & Y11

Balancing equations is not a talent. It's a procedure. Students who find it hard are almost always doing it by trial and error — changing a number, counting everything again, changing a different number, and losing track. Students who find it easy are following an order.

Here is the order.

The rule everything rests on

Atoms are never created or destroyed in a chemical reaction. They are only rearranged. So whatever atoms go in on the left must come out on the right — same elements, same numbers.

The one thing you may never do

  • You may change the big numbers in front of a formula. Those are coefficients.
  • You may never change the small numbers inside a formula. Those are subscripts, and changing one turns the substance into a different substance. H₂O is water. H₂O₂ is bleach.

The method, in five steps

  1. Write the equation with correct formulae. Get this wrong and nothing after it can be right.
  2. Count every atom of every element on both sides. Write the tally down — do not hold it in your head.
  3. Balance metals first.
  4. Then balance non-metals, leaving hydrogen and oxygen until last.
  5. Balance hydrogen, then oxygen. Re-count everything once at the end.

The reason hydrogen and oxygen go last is that they appear in the most compounds, so they shift every time you change something else. Everything else settles first, then they fall into place.

Worked example: burning methane

CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂OUnbalanced

Count: left has 1 C, 4 H, 2 O. Right has 1 C, 2 H, 3 O. Carbon is already balanced. Skip to hydrogen: we need 4 on the right, so put a 2 in front of H₂O.

CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂OHydrogen balanced

Re-count oxygen: the right now has 2 (in CO₂) + 2 (in 2H₂O) = 4. The left has 2. Put a 2 in front of O₂.

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂OBalanced ✓

Final check: C 1 = 1, H 4 = 4, O 4 = 4. Done, and it took three moves rather than thirty.

Worked example: a metal and an acid

Mg + HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂Unbalanced

Metals first: magnesium is 1 and 1, fine. Non-metals next: chlorine is 1 on the left and 2 on the right, so put a 2 in front of HCl. That gives 2 hydrogen on the left, and there are already 2 on the right in H₂.

Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂Balanced ✓

The three traps

Where the marks go

  • Changing a subscript. The single most common way to lose the whole mark.
  • Forgetting the diatomic elements. H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂ — they never appear alone.
  • Not re-counting at the end. Balancing oxygen often unbalances hydrogen. One final tally catches it.

State symbols

If the question asks for them, they are worth a mark on their own: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (aq) aqueous — dissolved in water — and (g) gas. The most common slip is writing (l) for something dissolved. Salt solution is (aq), not (l).

Practise these five

  1. Na + H₂O → NaOH + H₂
  2. Fe + O₂ → Fe₂O₃
  3. C₂H₆ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O
  4. Al + Cl₂ → AlCl₃
  5. CaCO₃ + HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂

Do all five with the method rather than by eye, and by the fifth it will have stopped feeling like a puzzle. That's the whole point of week two of the 4-week course.
