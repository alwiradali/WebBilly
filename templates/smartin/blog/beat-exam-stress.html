Confidence

“I’m just not a science person” — and why that’s almost never true

By Rod Martin5 min readGCSE Combined Science · Y10 & Y11

After twenty years of teaching science, I've heard that sentence from hundreds of students. Almost none of them were right.

“I'm not a science person” is very rarely a statement about ability. It is a conclusion a student has drawn from evidence — and the evidence is usually a specific, findable gap that appeared eighteen months earlier.

How it usually happens

A student misses a fortnight in Year 9. Or a topic is taught in a week that needed two. Or they were ill on the day the mole was introduced. The class moves on, because classes must. Everything built on top of that topic now feels slightly unstable, and the student cannot say why.

What they can say is that science is confusing and everyone else seems to get it. From there, “I'm not a science person” is not an unreasonable conclusion. It is just the wrong one.

A confidence problem and a knowledge gap look identical from the outside. Only one of them is actually the problem.

What the belief costs

  • It becomes a reason not to try, which produces exactly the results that confirm it.
  • It makes asking questions feel humiliating, so the gap never gets found.
  • It leaks into other subjects: “I'm bad at maths too”, usually next.
  • It closes down post-16 options at fifteen, on the basis of one bad term.

How to actually shift it

Reassurance doesn't work. Telling a student they're clever when their last mock says otherwise just teaches them you don't understand the situation. What works is evidence.

Four things that move the needle

  • Find the actual gap. Go back until the questions get easy, then come forward. It's usually closer than anyone expects.
  • Make progress visible. A topic they could not do on Monday and can do on Friday is worth more than any pep talk.
  • Separate knowing from performing. A lot of “I can't do science” is really “I can't do exams”, which is a much easier fix.
  • Change the language. “I haven't learned this yet” instead of “I can't do this”. It sounds small. It isn't.

What parents can do this week

  1. Ask which topic is hardest, not which subject. Specific questions get specific answers.
  2. Don't share your own science anxiety. “I was terrible at science too” is meant kindly and lands as permission to give up.
  3. Praise the process — the practice paper that got done — rather than the result.
  4. Get the gap found by someone who knows the specification. Four weeks is usually enough.

What changing looks like

It rarely arrives as a dramatic turnaround. It arrives as a student who stops saying “I don't get any of this” and starts saying “I don't get question 4”. That shift — from a whole subject to one question — is the moment the belief has broken, and it usually happens in the first fortnight.

Everything after that is just teaching.

That's the whole design of the 4-week course: find the gap, close it, and let the student see it close.
