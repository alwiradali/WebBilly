Biology

Bacteria vs viruses: the comparison examiners keep asking for

By Rod Martin6 min readGCSE Combined Science · Y10 & Y11

Infection and response comes up on every GCSE Combined Science paper, and one question comes up more often than any other: what is the difference between a bacterium and a virus? Students almost always know something. They almost as often write an answer that scores one mark out of three, because they describe what the two have in common instead of what separates them.

There are three major things that separate them — size, structure and reproduction — and two more that examiners love: how they make you ill, and how they're treated. Learn those five and this question stops being a question.

The comparison, in one table

The differenceBacteriaViruses
SizeAround 1,000 nm — visible with a light microscope.Around 20–300 nm — need an electron microscope.
StructureLiving cells: cell wall, membrane, cytoplasm, loose DNA. No nucleus.Not cells: genetic material in a protein coat. No cytoplasm, no membrane.
ReproductionBinary fission — as fast as every 20 minutes in ideal conditions.Cannot reproduce alone. They invade a host cell and force it to make copies.
Cause illness byProducing toxins that damage cells and tissues.Bursting the host cells they have taken over.
TreatmentAntibiotics kill them without harming body cells.Antibiotics do nothing. Antivirals are hard to make.
Named examplesSalmonella · GonorrhoeaMeasles · HIV · Tobacco mosaic virus

Why size is worth a mark on its own

A typical bacterium is roughly a hundred times bigger than a typical virus. That single fact explains two others: it's why bacteria can be seen with a school light microscope and viruses cannot, and it's why viruses can get inside a cell in the first place. If a question gives you a scale bar or a magnification, that is the examiner telling you size is the point.

Exam trap

  • “Viruses are smaller” is worth a mark. “Viruses are very small” usually isn't — comparisons need both sides.
  • Watch the units. Bacteria in µm, viruses in nm. 1 µm = 1,000 nm.

Structure: living cell vs not a cell at all

This is the difference that decides most of the others. A bacterium is a living cell — it has a cell wall, a cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and a single loop of DNA floating free (plus small rings called plasmids). It has no nucleus, which is why it's prokaryotic.

A virus has none of that. It is genetic material — DNA or RNA — wrapped in a protein coat, and nothing else. No cytoplasm. No membrane. No ribosomes. Which is exactly why it cannot do anything by itself.

If you remember one sentence: bacteria are cells that live. Viruses are instructions in a box that need a cell to run them.

Reproduction: the reason viruses need you

Bacteria reproduce by binary fission — the cell copies its DNA and splits in two. Under ideal conditions of warmth, moisture and nutrients that can happen every twenty minutes, which is why bacterial infections can escalate so quickly, and why the population graph in the exam is exponential.

Viruses cannot reproduce on their own at all. A virus attaches to a host cell, injects its genetic material, and hijacks the cell's machinery into building new virus particles. The cell eventually bursts, releasing them, and the cycle repeats. That bursting is what damages the body — the virus itself doesn't produce toxins.

Treatment: the highest-value mark in the topic

Antibiotics work on bacteria because they attack structures bacteria have and human cells don't — the cell wall, for example. A virus has none of those structures, and it spends its time inside your own cells, so a drug that killed it would have to get through your cell first. That is why antivirals are difficult to develop and why antibiotics are useless against a cold.

Examiners ask this as a “explain why antibiotics cannot be used to treat viral infections” question. The mark is not for saying they don't work. It is for saying viruses live inside body cells, so a drug that destroys the virus would damage the body cells too.

Answer this question in three moves

  • Name the difference — size, structure, or reproduction.
  • State both sides — never one. “Bacteria are… whereas viruses are…”
  • Add the consequence — the third mark is almost always “and therefore…”

Quick self-check

  1. Give two structures found in a bacterium but not in a virus.
  2. Explain why a virus cannot reproduce outside a host cell.
  3. A student says antibiotics will cure their flu. Explain why they are wrong.
  4. Name one bacterial and one viral disease from the specification.

If any of those made you hesitate, that's the gap — and it takes about twenty minutes to close properly. It's exactly the sort of thing week one of the 4-week course is for.
