Exam Technique

How to answer a 6-mark question — and stop losing easy marks

By Rod Martin7 min readGCSE Combined Science · Y10 & Y11

Six-mark questions frighten students more than any other part of the paper, and they are the single biggest source of recoverable marks. Recoverable, because most students who lose them are not short of knowledge. They are short of structure.

What is actually being marked

Extended-response questions are marked in bands, not by ticking individual facts. To reach the top band an answer normally needs to be relevant, detailed, and logically ordered. That last one is why a page of correct science written in a random order lands in the middle band.

You are not being marked on how much you know. You are being marked on how well you organise what you know.

Read the command word first

Everything else follows from this. Answering “explain” as though it said “describe” is the most common six-mark failure there is.

Command wordWhat it wantsTest
DescribeWhat happens — the observations, in order.No “because” needed.
ExplainWhy it happens — the reasons and mechanisms.Every point needs a “because”.
CompareSimilarities and differences, both sides in the same sentence.Use “whereas”.
EvaluateAdvantages, disadvantages, then a conclusion.Must end with a judgement.
SuggestApply what you know to a situation you've never seen.It isn't in your notes — that's deliberate.

The structure: plan, points, link

  1. Spend 60 seconds planning. Bullet four or five points in the margin. It feels like lost time. It is the opposite.
  2. Order them. Cause before effect. Beginning before end. Method before result.
  3. One idea per sentence. Long sentences hide the point the examiner is looking for.
  4. Use the science vocabulary. “Diffusion”, not “it spreads out”. Technical words are frequently the mark.
  5. Link back to the question in the last sentence. If it said “evaluate”, this is where the judgement goes.

Worked example

“Explain how the structure of the small intestine makes it efficient at absorbing digested food. (6 marks)”

Plan: villi → microvilli → surface area → thin wall → short diffusion distance → blood supply → concentration gradient → length.

The answer, structured

  • The inner surface is folded into villi, and each villus is covered in microvilli.
  • This gives a very large surface area, so more molecules can be absorbed at once.
  • The wall of each villus is one cell thick, so the diffusion distance is short.
  • Each villus has a rich blood supply, which carries absorbed molecules away.
  • This keeps the concentration inside the villus low, maintaining a steep concentration gradient.
  • The small intestine is also several metres long, so food is in contact with the surface for longer.

Six points, six marks, every one of them a “because”. Notice there is no introduction and no conclusion padding — nothing in that answer is there to fill space.

What loses marks

  • Repeating the question back as an opening sentence.
  • Listing facts with no link between them.
  • Vague language: “it gets bigger”, “it goes faster”, “it's better”.
  • Answering a different question — usually the one you revised.
  • Running out of space because the first two points took eight lines.

Timing

A six-mark question is worth roughly six minutes: one to plan, four to write, one to check. Students who skip the plan usually spend eight minutes and score less. Practising with a timer is the only way this becomes automatic.
