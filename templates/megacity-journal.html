HMO · Licensing

Does my property need
an HMO licence?

This is the question we are asked more than any other, and the honest answer is that it depends on two separate things: what the national rules say, and what your particular council has decided locally. Landlords get caught out by the second one.

First, is it an HMO at all?

A property is a House in Multiple Occupation when at least three tenants live there forming more than one household, and they share a kitchen, bathroom or toilet. “Household” means a family or a couple, so three friends renting a three-bedroom house together is an HMO, while a family of five is not. That definition catches a lot of ordinary shared houses in Fallowfield, Rusholme and Salford that landlords think of simply as a let.

Then, does it need a licence?

There are three separate licensing regimes and they stack:

  • Mandatory HMO licensing. Applies across England to any HMO occupied by five or more people forming two or more households. The old “three storeys or more” condition was removed in 2018, so a five-person HMO in a two-storey terrace needs a licence just as much as one in a large townhouse.
  • Additional licensing. A council can extend licensing to smaller HMOs, typically three or four occupants, in areas it designates. Manchester and Salford have both used these powers.
  • Selective licensing. The one that surprises people. In a designated selective licensing area every private rented property needs a licence, including a single family let with one tenant. Designations are made ward by ward and they expire and get renewed, so an area that was not covered when you bought may be covered now.

What it costs to get wrong

Operating an unlicensed property that requires a licence is a criminal offence. A council can issue a civil penalty of up to £30,000 per offence as an alternative to prosecution, and prosecution carries an unlimited fine. Separately, the tenants or the council can apply for a Rent Repayment Order requiring you to repay up to twelve months’ rent. On a room-let HMO at £450 a room that is a genuinely serious number.

The conditions that come with the licence

A licence is not just a fee. It brings minimum room sizes, broadly 6.51m² for one person over ten, and 10.22m² where two people share, along with requirements on kitchen and bathroom provision for the number of occupants, fire doors and detection, and a fit-and-proper-person test on whoever manages the property. Bringing a house up to that standard is usually where the real cost and the real delay sit, not the application form.

What we do about it. Before we market an HMO we check the property against the current designations for its exact address, tell you which regime applies, and price the works needed to meet the conditions. We handle applications and renewals, and we keep the licence expiry in the same diary as your gas and electrical certificates. If you are buying, ask us before you exchange. It is a far cheaper conversation then.

Compliance

Awaab’s Law, damp
and mould.

Damp and mould used to be treated as a maintenance annoyance and, too often, blamed on the tenant’s drying habits. That era is over. There are now fixed timescales attached to it, and the burden of proving you acted sits with the landlord.

Where the law came from

Awaab Ishak was a two-year-old boy in Rochdale who died in 2020 after prolonged exposure to mould in his family’s home. The rules that carry his name were introduced for social housing and have been extended to the private rented sector through the Renters’ Rights Act. The principle is simple: once a serious hazard is reported, the clock starts.

What it means in practice

You are required to investigate a reported damp or mould hazard within a set period, tell the tenant in writing what you found, and begin the remedial work within a further set period, with far shorter deadlines where the hazard poses a significant risk to health. The exact timescales are being phased in, and the sensible working assumption is days, not months.

Alongside this sits the Homes (Fitness for Human Habitation) Act, which already lets a tenant take a landlord to court directly over a property that is unfit, and the Housing Health and Safety Rating System, which councils use to assess hazards including damp, excess cold and fire risk.

The part landlords underestimate: evidence

Almost every dispute we have seen turns on records rather than facts. If a tenant reports mould and you send someone the same week, the difference between a resolved repair and a claim is whether you can show when it was reported, when you attended, what you found and what you did. A phone call that nobody wrote down is worth nothing eighteen months later.

What usually actually fixes it

Most of the damp we deal with in Manchester’s older housing stock is condensation rather than a leak or rising damp, and the fix is normally ventilation and heating rather than repainting. Extractor fans that actually run on a humidity sensor, trickle vents, insulation, and dealing with cold bridging solve far more cases than mould wash ever has. Where it is a genuine defect, a failed gutter, a slipped slate, a bridged damp course, painting over it simply restarts the clock.

What we do about it. Every report is logged with a date and a photograph, the tenant gets written acknowledgement, and the contractor visit is recorded against the property. Routine inspections include damp checks, so we usually find it before the tenant has to report it. You get the record whether or not it ever matters, and if it does, you have it.

Voids · Yield

What a void month
really costs.

Landlords tend to price a void as one month’s lost rent. It is reliably more than that, and understanding the true figure changes how you think about asking rent.

The arithmetic

Take a property let at £1,000 a month. An empty month is not £1,000. It is the rent, plus council tax which becomes your liability the moment the tenancy ends, plus standing charges on the utilities, plus the cost of re-letting, cleaning, any touch-up decorating, and the re-let fee. Realistically you are looking at £1,300 to £1,500 for that month.

Now compare that with pricing. If you hold out for £1,050 instead of £1,000 and it takes an extra month to let, you have spent roughly £1,400 to gain £600 over the following year. You do not get back to where you started until month twenty-three, and that assumes the tenant stays that long.

Why over-pricing is the most expensive mistake

This is the single most common way we see landlords lose money, and it rarely feels like a mistake at the time. An ambitious asking rent produces fewer enquiries, and the enquiries it does produce are more likely to be from applicants who cannot pass referencing but are willing to agree a high figure. So you wait longer and your applicant pool gets worse. A property that is priced correctly typically lets from the first weekend of viewings.

Timing matters more than most people think

Manchester’s rental market has a rhythm. Student and young-professional demand concentrates heavily around summer; family lets move more in spring. A tenancy that ends in December will sit empty longer than the same property ending in June. When we set up a tenancy, we look at when it would come back to market and, where it makes sense, we structure it so renewal lands in a strong month rather than a dead one.

Keeping a good tenant is cheaper than finding a new one

Now that all tenancies are periodic and a tenant can leave on two months’ notice, retention is worth more than it used to be. Answering the phone, fixing things quickly and being reasonable at renewal are not soft skills. They are the cheapest void insurance available. A tenant who stays three years instead of one saves you two re-lets and two voids.

What we do about it. We price from lettings we have actually agreed nearby this year, not from portal asking prices, which are a record of what people hoped for. We start marketing before the current tenancy ends where the tenant allows access, and we tell you honestly if we think your figure will cost you a void.

Deposits

Winning a deposit dispute
starts on move-in day.

Deposit adjudication is decided on evidence, not on who sounds more reasonable. Almost every claim we have seen fail, failed because of something that was or was not done at the start of the tenancy.

The rules you have to get right first

The deposit must be protected in a government-approved scheme within 30 days of receipt, and the prescribed information must be served on the tenant within the same 30 days. Miss either and a tenant can ask the county court to order a penalty of between one and three times the deposit. Because each renewal is treated as a separate tenancy, a deposit that was never protected can attract that penalty more than once.

The burden of proof is on you

At the end of the tenancy the deposit belongs to the tenant unless you can demonstrate a loss. That is the sentence to remember. The adjudicator is not there to judge whether the property looks tired. They are there to compare a documented condition at the start with a documented condition at the end, and award the difference.

What a defensible inventory looks like

  • Dated and signed. Photographs with a date, and a written schedule the tenant has signed or been given a proper opportunity to comment on.
  • Specific, not general. “Kitchen, good condition” is worthless. “Worktop to left of hob, 4cm scorch mark” wins claims.
  • Meter readings and keys. Recorded at both ends.
  • Cleanliness recorded. Photographed at check-in, because “returned dirty” only means something relative to how it was handed over.
  • A matching check-out. Same rooms, same angles, same level of detail.

Fair wear and tear, and betterment

You cannot charge a tenant for the property ageing. Carpet thinning in a hallway over three years is wear; a cigarette burn is damage. And you cannot end up better off than you started: if a six-year-old carpet with a nine-year expected life is ruined, you are entitled to the remaining value, not a brand new carpet. Adjudicators apply this consistently, and claims that ignore it get reduced.

Where landlords most often lose

No inventory, or one with no photographs. Cleaning charged without check-in evidence. Invoices produced for work that was never actually done. Claiming full replacement cost for an old item. And deductions that were never explained to the tenant before the dispute was raised. Adjudicators notice when a claim appears only after the tenant asked for their money back.

What we do about it. Every managed tenancy starts with a dated photographic inventory and schedule of condition, and ends with a matching check-out report. Deposits go to the DPS inside the statutory window with the prescribed information served. If there is a deduction to claim, the evidence for it was gathered months before anyone needed it.
