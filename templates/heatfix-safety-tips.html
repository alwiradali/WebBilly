None of this replaces a proper check by a registered engineer, but knowing what to look for, and what to do first, genuinely matters.

If you can smell gas right now: open the doors and windows. Turn the gas off at the meter if you can reach it safely. Don't touch any light switch, plug or appliance. Don't smoke or light anything. Get everyone out, and from outside call the National Gas Emergency Service on 0800 111 999: free, 24 hours a day. Ring us afterwards, not first.

Carbon monoxide: the six signs

Carbon monoxide has no smell, no taste and no colour. It is produced when a gas appliance burns fuel incompletely: a blocked flue, a cracked heat exchanger, a badly fitted boiler. These are the warnings a house gives you:

  1. Headaches, dizziness, nausea, breathlessness or sudden tiredness, especially if they get better when you leave the house and come back when you return.
  2. Everyone in the household feeling ill at the same time, pets included.
  3. A lazy yellow or orange flame where it should be crisp blue.
  4. Dark, sooty staining or yellow-brown scorch marks on or around an appliance.
  5. Pilot lights that keep blowing out.
  6. Condensation building up on the windows of the room with the appliance in it.

If you suspect carbon monoxide, get out, open the windows, and ring 0800 111 999. Then see a doctor and tell them you think it may be CO poisoning.

Fit an audible alarm, and check it

Buy one marked EN 50291, fit it in the same room as the appliance at roughly head height and about a metre away, and press the test button monthly. Any DIY shop has them, and they last five to seven years. Write the replacement date on it in marker when you fit it. A CO alarm is not a substitute for servicing the appliance; it's the backstop for when something goes wrong between services.

Check the flame

A healthy gas flame burns crisp blue, with well-defined cones. A soft yellow or orange flame that flickers and drifts means the appliance is not burning cleanly and needs looking at. That applies to a hob ring, a gas fire and the burner inside a boiler alike.

Don't block the air

Gas appliances need air to burn safely and a clear route for the exhaust. So:

  • Never block an air brick, vent or grille: not with furniture, not with insulation, not "just for winter".
  • Keep the outside flue terminal clear of shrubs, fence panels and anything stored against the wall.
  • Never dry washing on or against a boiler, gas fire or flue.
  • If you've had new windows, an extension or a wood burner fitted, tell whoever services your gas appliances. The ventilation calculations change.

Book the yearly service

An annual service by a Gas Safe registered engineer is the single most useful thing you can do. It catches a corroding heat exchanger, a partially blocked flue or a failing seal long before any of them become dangerous, and a boiler that's burning cleanly costs less to run. If you let the property, a gas safety check is a legal requirement every twelve months.

Simple things worth doing every autumn

  • Run the heating for twenty minutes before the cold arrives, so you find the problem in October rather than on Christmas Eve.
  • Check the boiler pressure. Cold, it should sit around 1 to 1.5 bar on the gauge.
  • Bleed any radiator that's cold at the top.
  • Turn the outside tap off at the isolator and drain it before the first frost.
  • Find your internal stop tap now and make sure it actually turns. The night of a burst pipe is a bad time to discover it's seized.

Never work on gas yourself. It is against the law for anyone who isn't Gas Safe registered to install or repair a gas appliance, and it isn't a rule invented to sell call-outs. It exists because the failures kill people quietly.
