Fitting a gas cooker: what the law actually says

Who is allowed to connect it, why the stability chain matters, and the tightness test that nobody watches being done.

← All posts

A gas cooker looks like the simplest appliance in the house — a hose, a bayonet, push it into the gap. It isn't, and it's one of the jobs where the rules exist for extremely obvious reasons.

Who can connect it

Only a Gas Safe registered engineer may install, connect or disconnect a gas cooker. Not the delivery driver, not a general handyman, not you. It's a legal requirement under the Gas Safety (Installation and Use) Regulations 1998, and it applies whether the cooker is brand new or second-hand.

Registration is also categorised. An engineer qualified for boilers isn't automatically qualified for cookers — the ID card lists the specific appliance types. Worth a glance at the card, not just the fact there is one.

What a proper installation includes

  • The right hose and bayonet. Correct length, correct rating, and routed so it can't touch a hot surface or get trapped when the cooker is pushed back. The bayonet must be positioned so the hose hangs in a gentle downward loop, not stretched or kinked.
  • A stability chain or bracket. A freestanding cooker must be restrained so it can't tip forward if someone leans on an open oven door — which is exactly what happens when a child climbs on it. This is the item most commonly missing on cookers fitted by someone who shouldn't have.
  • Correct clearances. Distance to side walls, to any overhead cupboard or extractor, and to combustible surfaces, all to the manufacturer's figures.
  • A tightness test. The installation is pressure-tested with a manometer to prove there is no leak anywhere on the pipework, and the working pressure is checked.
  • A ventilation check. Room volume and openable window, and any purpose provided vent, checked against the appliance's requirements.
  • A combustion check on completion — flames burning crisp blue, no yellow tipping, and the oven and grill run up to temperature.

The things that get flagged as unsafe

On cooker jobs the faults we find most often are: no stability chain, a hose that's too short and pulling on the bayonet, a hose resting against the back of the oven, a cooker pushed in so far the hose is crushed, and an old bayonet that no longer seals when the plug is pulled. Every one of those was fitted by someone in a hurry.

Range cookers

Bigger cookers usually need a larger gas supply than a standard 15mm run will deliver, and the pipework often has to be upgraded before the appliance will work properly. Symptoms of an undersized supply are low flames on the back rings when everything is running, and an oven that takes far too long to come up. It's worth mentioning the model when you ring, so the right pipe comes on the van.

If you're buying a cooker: arrange the connection separately and don't rely on the retailer's delivery team to do it. Book the disconnect of the old one and the connection of the new one for the same visit — it's one job, and you're not left without a cooker overnight.
